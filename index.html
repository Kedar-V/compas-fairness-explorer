Can a risk score be fair to everyone at once?

An interactive look at the COMPAS debate (ProPublica vs. Northpointe) for IDS 704, Week 6. All the data is made up, and everything runs in your browser.

How this works

Imagine a tool that gives each defendant a risk score from 0 to 1. I built it so the scores mean the same thing for everyone: of the people who score 0.7, about 70% are re-arrested, in both groups. That was Northpointe's idea of fair. The two groups differ in one way only, the share of people who get re-arrested. A judge then labels everyone above a cutoff score as "high risk."

In the real COMPAS data, roughly 39% of white defendants and 51% of Black defendants were re-arrested within two years. Those are the starting numbers below. I call the groups A and B so the idea isn't tied to one dataset. One caveat: being re-arrested depends partly on where police patrol, and I come back to that at the bottom.

My view

The COMPAS disagreement is a problem because deciding what counts as fair is a question of values, but it usually gets treated as a technical question. When the two groups have different re-arrest rates, a tool can't both wrongly flag the same share of people in each group and have its flags be right equally often. So someone has to decide which group takes which kind of mistake. I think that decision belongs with judges and courts, because they use the score and answer for what it does to a defendant. As a data scientist, that leaves me a smaller job: show them the trade-off clearly. I would report each group's mistake rates, say which kind of fairness the tool meets, and say which one it gives up. I don't think I can quietly pick one and hand over a single risk number. Where my responsibility ends after that is something I'm still working out.

Why this matters

  • Both sides used real data, and each was right by its own definition. ProPublica compared how often each group was wrongly flagged. Northpointe compared how often a high-risk flag turned out to be right. This page shows the two pull against each other whenever the groups have different re-arrest rates.
  • Try it: pick "Even out wrongful flags" and look at how often a flag is right. Then pick "Even out how often a flag is right" and look at wrongful flags. The gaps only shrink together if both groups have the same re-arrest rate, or if the scores separate people almost perfectly (a very high spread).
  • Choosing is a values decision, not a technical one. A wrongful flag could mean tougher supervision for someone who would not have been re-arrested. A missed case is a public-safety cost. Who decides which mistake to accept?
  • Using a different cutoff for each group has its own fairness problem. It treats two defendants with the same score differently, which is unfair in how each person is treated and in the process, even though it evens out the mistakes.
  • The data is a caveat too. The label here is "re-arrested," which depends on where police patrol. If that label is biased, a score that is fair by its own measure inherits the bias, so none of these controls fix it.

Sources: Angwin et al., ProPublica (2016); Kleinberg, Mullainathan & Raghavan (2016) and Chouldechova (2017) on why the two kinds of fairness can't both hold. Model: each group's scores follow a Beta curve (a flexible curve between 0 and 1), set up so the scores mean the same thing for everyone.
