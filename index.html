Can a risk score be fair to everyone at once?

An interactive take on the COMPAS debate (ProPublica vs. Northpointe) for IDS 704, Week 6. Everything is synthetic and runs in your browser.

The setup

Imagine a risk tool that gives every defendant a score from 0 to 1. I built it to be perfectly calibrated: among people who score 0.7, exactly 70% are re-arrested, in both groups. That is Northpointe's definition of fair. The groups differ only in their overall re-arrest rate (the base rate). Then a judge flags everyone above a threshold as "high risk."

Base rates in the real COMPAS data were roughly 39% (white) vs 51% (Black) re-arrest within two years, which are the defaults below. Group labels are generic so the point isn't tied to any one dataset. Note that "re-arrest" is itself shaped by policing patterns, a caveat I come back to at the bottom.

Why this matters

  • Both sides were using real data and both were right about their own definition. ProPublica compared error rates (false positives). Northpointe compared calibration (precision). This page shows they pull against each other whenever base rates differ.
  • Try it: pick "Equalize false positive rates," then look at precision. Then pick "Equalize precision" and look at false positives. Only if you set both base rates equal (or make scores perfectly decisive with a very high spread) do the gaps shrink together.
  • Choosing is a values decision, not a technical one. A false positive could mean stricter supervision for someone who would not have been re-arrested. A false negative is a public-safety cost. Who decides which error to tolerate?
  • Different thresholds per group has its own fairness problem. Equalizing errors this way means treating two defendants with the same score differently, which hits procedural and interpersonal fairness even as it improves distributive fairness.
  • The data itself is a caveat. The label here is "re-arrested," which depends on where police patrol. If the label is biased, a "perfectly calibrated" score inherits that bias, so none of these knobs fix it.

Sources: Angwin et al., ProPublica (2016); Kleinberg, Mullainathan & Raghavan (2016) and Chouldechova (2017) on the impossibility result. Model: scores ~ Beta(p·k, (1−p)·k), calibrated by construction.
